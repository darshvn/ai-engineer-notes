How to use these notes

These are deliberately not written to be re-read. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate re-reading and highlighting as low utility — they produce fluency, which your brain misreads as understanding. Retrieval practice and spacing are the only two techniques that scored high utility.

So: read a section once, cover it, and answer that section's question in the retrieval bank at the bottom from memory. Then check. The gap between what you can produce and what you recognise is the only honest measure of what stuck.

Each concept is given twice on purpose — once in prose, once as a diagram. If the prose slides off, look at the picture; if the picture is opaque, the paragraph above it says the same thing in words. Use whichever lane lands, then come back for the other one.

01

What “language modeling” actually means

Roadmap item · 1.0 hr · done

A language model is a probability distribution over sequences of text. That is the entire definition. Everything else — transformers, GPUs, RLHF — is engineering in service of that one job.

Hand it any string and it returns how likely that string is. "The cat sat on the mat" scores high. "The cat sat on the thermodynamics" scores low. Nothing more mystical is being claimed.

Then comes the move that makes it tractable. Modeling the probability of a whole document directly is hopeless, so you factor it with the chain rule of probability:

P(w₁ w₂ w₃ … wₙ) = P(w₁) · P(w₂|w₁) · P(w₃|w₁w₂) · … · P(wₙ|w₁…wₙ₋₁)

Read the right-hand side closely. Every term is the same question asked over and over: given everything so far, what comes next? That collapse — from “model all of language” down to “be good at one conditional” — is the founding trick of the entire field.

The lineage follows that one objective forward: n-gram models (count occurrences, assume only the last few words matter) → neural LMs (learn a smooth function instead of counting) → transformers (condition on thousands of tokens at once). The objective never changed. Only the ability to condition on long context did.

Misconception to kill now

“The model stores facts and looks them up.”

There is no lookup table. There are ~10¹¹ floating-point numbers that shape a distribution. Facts fall out as a side effect of compressing a corpus well. This is why the model can be fluently, confidently wrong: it isn't retrieving a bad record, it's sampling from a distribution that was never a database in the first place.

Engineer's takeaway

Because the output is a distribution, hallucination is not a failure mode bolted onto the system. It is the system running exactly as designed. Every mitigation you'll build later — RAG, grounding, structured output, evals — is a way of constraining a probabilistic process, never of making it deterministic.

02

Next-token prediction intuition

Roadmap item · 1.0 hr · done

The label is the next token in the text itself. Nobody annotated anything — and that is the whole reason this scaled when supervised learning couldn't.

The training loop, concretely:

  1. Take a document. Chop it into tokens.
  2. At every position i, predict token i from tokens 1…i-1.
  3. Score against the actual token. Loss = -log P(correct token). Confident and right → near-zero loss. Confident and wrong → enormous loss.
  4. Backpropagate. Nudge the parameters. Repeat on the order of 10¹³ times.

That is what self-supervised means: the internet was already a labeled dataset, we just hadn't noticed. And notice what the loss punishes hardest — not being wrong, but being confidently wrong. Calibration is trained in from the first step.

Now the asymmetry that trips up almost everyone, because it drives most of your future infrastructure bill:

Predict before you read on

The model is generating a sentence. Does it decide the ending before writing the beginning?

No. There is zero lookahead. "The capital of France is" produces a distribution over ~100k tokens where " Paris" might hold 0.92 of the mass. One token is sampled, appended, and the model runs again on the longer string. It never plans and it can never retract.

The analogy worth keeping: an improv performer who must speak one word at a time, is forbidden from taking anything back, but has read everything ever written. Coherence is not planned — it emerges because the entire prefix conditions every single next choice.

03

Why next-token prediction produces reasoning-like behavior

Roadmap item · 1.0 hr · not started

The training corpus is far larger than the parameter count. Memorisation is physically unavailable. So gradient descent is forced to compress — and the cheapest compression of text-about-the-world turns out to be a model of the world.

This is the conceptual jump of the whole section. Four arguments, stacked.

(a) The compression argument — the load-bearing one. To predict the last page of a murder mystery you must model motive, alibi and narrative structure. To predict the token after "2 + 2 =", learning addition costs fewer parameters than memorising every integer pair. Reasoning is instrumentally useful for prediction, so prediction pressure manufactures it.

(b) Text is the fossil record of human reasoning. People wrote their reasoning chains down — proofs, debugging logs, legal arguments, code reviews. Predicting those tokens well requires reproducing the process that generated them.

(c) Prediction is a universal task. Translation is “complete English: … French:”. Summarisation, classification, code generation — all expressible as text completion. One loss function is quietly optimising across every task at once.

(d) Chain-of-thought is mechanical, not magical. A single forward pass has fixed compute. When the model writes intermediate steps, each generated token feeds back as input — converting a one-pass problem into an N-pass problem. It is literally buying more compute per question, using its own output as external working memory.

Misconception — both directions

“It's just autocomplete” and “it genuinely thinks” are wrong in the same way: both confuse the training objective with the learned solution.

Evolution optimises exactly one thing — reproductive fitness. It produced eyes, birdsong, and parental love. Simple objective, rich instrumental machinery. Next-token prediction is the objective. What got built to satisfy it is a separate question, and the honest answer is: functions that behave like reasoning on distributions resembling training data, and grow brittle off-distribution.

Engineer's takeaway

If a task needs multiple steps of computation, give the model room to write them down. Squeezing a hard problem into a terse one-line answer is not efficiency — you are capping how much compute it is allowed to spend.

04

Emergence & scaling laws

Roadmap item · 1.0 hr · not started

Loss is predictable. Capability is not. Nearly every practical mistake in this area comes from assuming the second follows from the first.

Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022): test loss falls as a power law in parameters (N), data (D) and compute (C). Plot it on log-log axes and you get a straight line, holding for many orders of magnitude.

The consequence is remarkable: you can train a family of small models, fit the line, and forecast the loss of a model you have not trained. That predictability is the only reason a lab will commit nine figures to a single training run.

Chinchilla is the result worth memorising. For a fixed compute budget there is an optimal split between model size and data — roughly 20 tokens per parameter. Before this, models were badly undertrained: GPT-3 was 175B parameters on ~300B tokens. Chinchilla at 70B beat Gopher at 280B purely by rebalancing toward data.

The modern twist matters for your job. Chinchilla optimises training cost. In production you pay inference cost forever. So labs now deliberately train far past the compute-optimal point — overtraining a small model to get one that is cheap to serve. That is the entire reason a genuinely strong 8B model exists.

The nuance most roadmaps skip

Schaeffer et al. (2023), “Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?” — much of the apparent discontinuity is an artifact of the metric, not a phase change in the model.

The takeaway isn't “emergence is fake.” It's sharper and far more useful: the underlying capability improves smoothly and predictably, while your measurement of it can be a step function. Which means a benchmark that reads 0% today can read 60% at the next scale-up with nothing surprising having happened inside the model.

Genuinely useful examples of scale-dependent behavior: multi-digit arithmetic, reliable instruction following, and benefiting from chain-of-thought at all — CoT actively hurts small models, which produce plausible-looking steps that don't constrain the answer.

Engineer's takeaway

Never extrapolate behavior — evaluate it. “The next model will be like this one but 10% better” is precisely the wrong mental model. Capabilities arrive in lumps, and so do regressions. Keep an eval suite that you can re-run the day a new model ships.

05

Foundation models vs task-specific models

Roadmap item · 0.5 hr · not started

“Foundation model” describes breadth of pretraining and adaptability — not parameter count. A 3B model pretrained broadly is one. A 20B model trained on protein sequences for a single task is not.

The old paradigm: one model per task. A sentiment classifier, an NER tagger, a translator. Each needs its own labeled dataset, training run, deployment and monitoring. Each shatters on domain shift.

The new paradigm: one heavily pretrained model, many tasks, adapted cheaply.

The term comes from Stanford CRFM (Bommasani et al., 2021). Three defining properties: trained on broad data, at scale, via self-supervision — and adaptable downstream. The metaphor was chosen carefully: it is a base others build on, and its cracks propagate into every building above it. Homogenisation is simultaneously the value proposition and the systemic risk. When one model underpins ten thousand products, its blind spots become everyone's blind spots at once.

When task-specific still wins — and it genuinely does: narrow scope, plentiful labels, and a hard latency or cost ceiling. A distilled 100M-parameter spam classifier answering in 3ms beats a frontier model at 800ms and several hundred times the cost, and it will never be prompt-injected. The mature pattern is to use the foundation model to find product-market fit, then distil the hot path.

Misconception

“Foundation model just means big model.” Size is a consequence, not the definition. What makes something a foundation model is that it was pretrained broadly enough to be pointed at tasks nobody had in mind when it was trained.

06

Generative vs representation models

Roadmap item · 0.75 hr · not started

One asks “what comes next?” and returns tokens. The other asks “what does this mean?” and returns a vector. Every real RAG system runs both, and confusing them is the most common beginner architecture error.

 GenerativeRepresentation
Outputa sequence of tokensone fixed-length vector
Architecturedecoder-onlyencoder-only
Attention maskcausal — see left onlybidirectional — see everything
Objectivenext-token predictionmasked-token / contrastive
ExamplesGPT · Claude · Llama · MistralBERT · text-embedding-3 · e5 · bge
Cost profileexpensive, streamed, serialcheap, batchable, cacheable

The mechanical difference is close to a single line of code: the attention mask. Apply a causal mask so each token attends only to its left, and you have a generative model. Remove it so every token attends to the whole sequence, and you have a representation model. That plus the training objective is essentially the whole split.

Contrastive training deserves its own name, because it is a genuinely different objective. You show the model pairs, pull semantically similar ones together in vector space, and push dissimilar ones apart. You are not predicting tokens at all — you are sculpting a geometry in which cosine distance means semantic distance.

Misconception

“I'll just grab the LLM's hidden states and use them as embeddings.”

You can, and they will be mediocre. Causal masking gives you a half-blind representation, and nothing in next-token training optimised that vector space for cosine similarity. Purpose-built embedding models — often a hundred times smaller — beat frontier LLMs at retrieval.

Worth knowing about the third family: encoder-decoder (T5, BART). The encoder reads bidirectionally, the decoder generates causally, and cross-attention bridges them. Largely displaced by decoder-only for general-purpose LLMs, but still strong for fixed input→output transforms like translation. Your roadmap picks this up in §2 under “Encoder-only vs Decoder-only vs Encoder-Decoder.”

Retrieval bank

Cover the notes. Write each answer out before you open it — retrieval that you don't check can reinforce errors, and retrieval that feels easy isn't doing anything.

State what a language model is in one sentence, without using the word “predict.”
A probability distribution over sequences of text. Given any string, it returns how likely that string is. The chain rule then turns that into a repeated conditional, which is where prediction enters — but the distribution is the definition.
Why does the chain rule make language modeling tractable?
It factors the joint probability of an entire sequence into a product of one-step conditionals, each of the form P(wᵢ | w₁…wᵢ₋₁). One impossible problem becomes one easy problem repeated — and it's the same problem every time, so a single learned function covers all of it.
What does the loss function punish hardest, and why does that matter?
-log P(correct token) explodes when the model is confident and wrong, not merely wrong. Calibration is trained in from step one — which is also why log-probabilities are a meaningful confidence signal at inference time, unlike a model just saying “I'm sure.”
Give the compression argument for why next-token prediction yields reasoning.
The corpus is far bigger than the parameter count, so memorisation is physically unavailable. Gradient descent must compress, and the cheapest compression of text-about-the-world is a model of the world. Learning addition is cheaper than storing every integer pair; modelling motive is cheaper than storing every mystery novel.
Mechanically — not motivationally — why does chain-of-thought improve answers?
A forward pass has fixed compute. The model's own output is the only thing it can add to its own input, so writing intermediate tokens is the only way to spend more than one pass on a problem. N written steps buys N passes. That's also why the reasoning has to be visible: there is no hidden scratchpad.
What did Chinchilla change, and why do labs now deliberately ignore it?
It showed that for a fixed compute budget there's an optimal size-to-data ratio, roughly 20 tokens per parameter — earlier models were badly undertrained. Labs now overtrain small models past that point because Chinchilla optimises training cost, while production pays inference cost forever.
Someone says a capability “emerged” at 70B. What's the sharpest sceptical question?
“What metric?” Exact-match accuracy is a step function by construction — zero until every digit is right. Schaeffer et al. (2023) showed much apparent emergence is a measurement artifact; the same model measured by per-token log-probability improves smoothly. The real lesson: loss is extrapolable, downstream capability is not.
What actually makes something a foundation model?
Broad pretraining data, at scale, via self-supervision, and adaptability to tasks nobody had in mind during training. Not parameter count. A broadly-pretrained 3B model qualifies; a 20B protein model trained for one task does not.
You have a spam classifier running at 50k requests/second. Foundation model or task-specific?
Task-specific, almost certainly distilled. Narrow scope, abundant labels, and a hard latency and cost ceiling — the three conditions where the old paradigm still wins outright. Use the foundation model to build the labelled set and prove the product, then distil the hot path.
What's the single architectural knob separating generative from representation models?
The attention mask. Causal (each token sees only its left context) gives you a decoder-only generative model; bidirectional (every token sees the whole sequence) gives you an encoder-only representation model. Objective differs too — next-token versus masked-token or contrastive — but the mask is the mechanical split.
You need semantic search over 2M documents plus natural-language answers. How many models, which kind?
Two. An embedding (representation) model to vectorise the corpus and each query for retrieval, and a generative model to write the answer from the retrieved chunks. Different cost curves, different failure modes: the embedding half is cheap, batchable and cacheable; the generative half is what shows up on the bill.
Why are an LLM's hidden states a poor substitute for a real embedding model?
Causal masking means the representation of any token never saw what came after it, and nothing in next-token training shaped that vector space for cosine similarity. A purpose-built contrastively-trained encoder, often 100× smaller, retrieves better.
